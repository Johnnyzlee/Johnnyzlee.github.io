Problem 1

You have three non-uniform ropes. Each rope takes exactly eight minutes to burn completely from one end, but it does not burn at a uniform rate. How can you measure exactly seven minutes?

Show answer
  1. At time 0, light Rope 1 at both ends. Light Ropes 2 and 3 at one end each.
  2. Rope 1 burns out after 4 minutes. At that moment, light the other end of Rope 2.
  3. Rope 2 now burns from both ends and burns out 2 minutes later, at the 6-minute mark. Then light the other end of Rope 3.
  4. Rope 3 has 2 minutes of one-ended burning time left at that point. Burning it from both ends makes it finish in 1 more minute.

Therefore, Rope 3 burns out exactly 7 minutes after the start.

Problem 2 ★

Alice and Bob play two rounds of rock-paper-scissors. Alice is not allowed to play scissors. If Bob plays rock or paper in the first round, he must play scissors in the second round. In each round, the loser pays the winner $1. Both players are perfectly rational and use optimal mixed strategies. What is Alice's expected total payoff?

Show answer

Work backward from the second round.

  • If Bob plays rock or paper in Round 1, he is forced to play scissors in Round 2. Alice responds with rock and earns 1 in the second round.
  • If Bob plays scissors in Round 1, his Round 2 move is unrestricted. In that one-round game, Alice optimally plays rock with probability 1/3 and paper with probability 2/3. The value to Alice is -1/3.

Including these continuation values, Alice's total-payoff matrix for Round 1 is:

Bob: RockBob: PaperBob: Scissors
Alice: Rock102/3
Alice: Paper21-4/3

Let p be the probability that Alice plays rock in Round 1. Bob's rock column is never better for him than his paper column, so the two relevant expected payoffs are 1 - p against paper and 2p - 4/3 against scissors.

Alice maximizes the smaller of these two quantities by making them equal:

1 - p = 2p - 4/3,   so   p = 7/9.

The resulting game value is 1 - 7/9 = 2/9. Therefore, Alice's expected total payoff is +2/9.

At equilibrium, Alice plays rock with probability 7/9 in Round 1, while Bob plays paper with probability 2/3 and scissors with probability 1/3.

Problem 3

A fair coin is tossed repeatedly. Stop as soon as either two tails occur consecutively or the total number of heads is two greater than the total number of tails. What is the expected number of tosses?

Show answer

Let the following variables denote the expected number of additional tosses from each non-terminal state:

  • A: heads minus tails is 0, and the previous toss is not a tail (this includes the initial state).
  • B: heads minus tails is 1, and the previous toss is a head.
  • C: heads minus tails is -1, and the previous toss is a tail.
  • D: heads minus tails is 0, and the previous toss is a tail.

A first-step analysis gives:

A = 1 + (B + C)/2,

B = 1 + D/2,

C = 1 + A/2,

D = 1 + B/2.

In the equations for B, C, and D, the omitted half-probability transition is a stopping event and therefore contributes no additional tosses.

The middle pair gives B = D = 2. Substituting C = 1 + A/2 into the first equation gives:

A = 1 + (2 + 1 + A/2)/2 = 5/2 + A/4.

Therefore, A = 10/3. The expected number of tosses is 10/3, or approximately 3.33.

Problem 4 ★

A stick is cut into n pieces by choosing n - 1 cut points independently and uniformly along its length. What is the probability that the pieces can form a non-degenerate n-gon? Assume n is at least 3.

Show answer

Normalize the total length to 1, and let the piece lengths be X1, ..., Xn. The pieces form a non-degenerate polygon if and only if the longest piece is shorter than the sum of all the others:

max(X1, ..., Xn) < 1/2.

The random length vector is uniformly distributed on the simplex X1 + ... + Xn = 1. For any fixed piece i, the region Xi >= 1/2 is a copy of the original simplex scaled by 1/2 in each of its n - 1 dimensions. Hence:

P(Xi >= 1/2) = (1/2)n - 1.

Two different pieces cannot both be longer than 1/2. Boundary cases have probability zero, so these n failure events are disjoint almost surely. Therefore:

P(failure) = n/2n - 1.

Thus, the required probability is 1 - n/2n - 1.

For example, when n = 3, this gives 1 - 3/4 = 1/4, the classical broken-stick result.

Problem 5 ★

The 2,026 numbers 1, 1/2, 1/3, ..., 1/2026 are written on a board. At each step, choose any two entries a and b, erase them, and write a + b + ab. Repeat until only one number remains. What is that number?

Show answer

Rewrite the operation in multiplicative form:

1 + (a + b + ab) = (1 + a)(1 + b).

Therefore, the product of 1 plus every number currently on the board is invariant: replacing a and b by a + b + ab replaces the two factors (1 + a)(1 + b) by an equal single factor.

If the final number is X, then:

1 + X = (1 + 1)(1 + 1/2)(1 + 1/3) ... (1 + 1/2026).

The product telescopes:

1 + X = (2/1)(3/2)(4/3) ... (2027/2026) = 2027.

Hence, X = 2026, regardless of which pairs are chosen.

Problem 6 ★

The integers 1 through 9 are arranged uniformly at random around a circle. What is the probability that the sum of the absolute differences between every pair of adjacent numbers, including the last and first, is 16?

Show answer

Fix 1 at a reference position and distinguish the clockwise direction. This gives 8! possible circular orders.

The circle contains two paths from 1 to 9. Along either path, the sum of the absolute differences is at least 9 - 1 = 8. Therefore, the total around the circle is at least 16.

Equality holds exactly when the numbers on each path increase monotonically from 1 to 9. Each of the seven intermediate numbers 2, 3, ..., 8 can be assigned to either path, and once those assignments are made, the order on both paths is forced. Hence there are 27 favorable circular orders.

P = 27/8! = 128/40320 = 1/315.

Therefore, the required probability is 1/315.

Problem 7 ★

The integers 1 through 8 are arranged in a uniformly random order. What is the probability that every entry after the first is either greater than all preceding entries or smaller than all preceding entries?

Show answer

Consider the last entry of a valid permutation. Because it must be outside the range of all seven preceding entries, it must be either the smallest number, 1, or the largest number, 8.

After removing that last entry, the remaining order must satisfy the same condition on seven numbers. Conversely, either the new minimum or the new maximum can be appended to any valid permutation of the other numbers.

Thus, if f(n) is the number of valid permutations of 1 through n, then:

f(n) = 2f(n - 1),   with   f(1) = 1.

Therefore, f(8) = 27 = 128. Since all 8! permutations are equally likely:

P = 27/8! = 128/40320 = 1/315.

The required probability is 1/315.

Problem 8 ★

A simple symmetric random walk starts at 0. At each step, it moves +1 or -1 with equal probability. Before it reaches 3 for the first time, what is the expected number of visits to -2? Count every landing on -2 as one visit.

Show answer

First, the probability of reaching -2 before 3 when starting from 0 is 3/5 by the gambler's ruin formula.

Now suppose the walk has reached -2. After each visit to -2, it avoids ever returning to -2 before hitting 3 only if it first moves to -1, which has probability 1/2, and then reaches 3 before -2. Starting from -1, that second probability is 1/5.

Therefore, the probability that a visit to -2 is the final visit before hitting 3 is:

(1/2)(1/5) = 1/10.

Conditional on reaching -2, the total number of visits to -2 is geometric with mean 10. Multiplying by the probability of reaching -2 at all gives:

E[number of visits to -2] = (3/5)(10) = 6.

Therefore, the expected number of visits to -2 before the first visit to 3 is 6.
